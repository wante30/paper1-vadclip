\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We use UCF-Crime~\cite{sultani2018real} (290 test videos) and XD-Violence~\cite{wu2020xdviolence} (800 test videos) with the CLIP features, splits, and frame-level ground truth released with VadCLIP. Following VadCLIP, we report frame-level AUC of $s^{(1)}$ on UCF-Crime and frame-level AP of $p$ on XD-Violence. Controlled experiments and ablations use a test-independent fixed-epoch protocol with matched seeds: every model is trained for a fixed number of epochs and the final checkpoint is evaluated. Each condition is run with seeds 234, 235, and 236, which share initialization and sample order across arms, and we report mean$\pm$SD, the paired difference per seed, and the number of positive seeds. We additionally evaluate the final method using VadCLIP's official training script.

Table~\ref{tab:main} places our results in the context of published CLIP-feature methods. The published rows are reproduced from VadCLIP and each follows its own training and model-selection protocol, so they give benchmark context rather than a controlled comparison; with our evaluator, VadCLIP's released checkpoints reach \ucfOfficial{} AUC on UCF-Crime and \xdOfficial{} AP on XD-Violence, the latter within \xdReadmeGap{} points of the \xdReadmeAp{} reported in its repository. Retrained under our test-independent protocol, VadCLIP reaches \ucfRetrained{} AUC and \xdBase{} AP, below its published values, which is expected since the official script selects checkpoints on the test set. Absolute numbers are therefore compared only within a block.

Our claims rest on the matched block. Against VadCLIP retrained under the same protocol and seeds, our method improves UCF-Crime AUC by \ucfGainMean{}$\pm$\ucfGainSd{} points and XD-Violence AP by \xdGainMean{}$\pm$\xdGainSd{} points, positive on every seed for both datasets. Because paired arms share initialization and sample order, the per-seed difference removes the variation common to a seed, which matters here: the seed-to-seed SD on XD-Violence ($\pm$\xdBaseSd{} for the baseline) is larger than the gain itself, so unpaired means alone would not separate the two arms.

Table~\ref{tab:controlled} reports both outputs on both datasets rather than only the headline metric of each. The UCF-Crime gain appears on AUC of $s^{(1)}$ and also on AP of the semantic output (\ucfApTwoGainMean{}$\pm$\ucfApTwoGainSd{} points), while on XD-Violence the gain is confined to the semantic output $p$: AP of $s^{(1)}$ changes by \xdApOneChangeMean{} points, consistent with Stage~2 leaving the classification pathway untouched. The two gains come from different designs. Stage~2 cannot move $s^{(1)}$, so the UCF-Crime gain is produced by Stage~1; Stage~1 alone changes XD-Violence AP by only \xdStageOneMean{} points, so the XD-Violence gain is produced by Stage~2. We therefore do not claim the stages reinforce each other on a single metric, and Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation} attributes each gain separately.

\textbf{Official training protocol.} To check that the XD-Violence gain is not an artifact of our protocol, we retrain both arms with VadCLIP's official script unchanged, which evaluates on the test set after every epoch and reloads the best checkpoint (Table~\ref{tab:official}). AP improves from \offBase{}$\pm$\offBaseSd{} to \offOurs{}$\pm$\offOursSd{}, a paired gain of \offGainMean{}$\pm$\offGainSd{} points on \offGainPos{} runs. Absolute AP is higher for both arms under this script than under our protocol, as test-set selection should make it, and the best single run reaches \offBestOurs{} AP; we report the three-seed mean as the result and give per-seed values here so the spread is visible. The gain survives the change of protocol, and we compare methods only within one protocol.

\subsection{Mechanism Ablations}
\label{sec:ablation}

\textbf{Where temporal variation is extracted.} Table~\ref{tab:ablation}(a) computes the same difference after aggregation, $D'_j = \lvert A_j - A_{j-1} \rvert$, with every other setting fixed, which isolates the position of the difference relative to pooling. Post-aggregation variation falls \emph{below} the baseline on every seed (\postBaseMean{} points), while pre-aggregation variation exceeds the post-aggregation variant by \prePostMean{}$\pm$\prePostSd{} AUC points on \prePostPos{} seeds, with an unusually small spread (Fig.~\ref{fig:paired}). The sign change is the informative part: adding a difference stream is not what helps, since the same stream computed one step later costs accuracy, presumably because differences of segment means add a noisy channel without adding information. What helps is the within-segment change that pooling would otherwise discard. This reading is consistent with where the effect appears: UCF-Crime averages \ucfBinSize{} snippets per segment against \xdBinSize{} on XD-Violence, so pooling discards more within-segment detail on UCF-Crime, which is also where Stage~1's gain concentrates.

\textbf{Where semantic optimization is applied.} Table~\ref{tab:ablation}(b) separates the objective from the extra training it requires. The control C runs the same three epochs from the same checkpoint with the same sample order and $\lambda=0$; the restricted objective adds \tMinusCMean{}$\pm$\tMinusCSd{} AP points over C on \tMinusCPos{} seeds, so continued training alone does not explain the gain. Applied directly to the retrained VadCLIP without Stage~1, Stage~2 improves AP by \bzTMean{}$\pm$\bzTSd{} points (\bzTPos{} seeds), reaching \xdBzT{}, which attributes essentially all of the XD-Violence gain to Stage~2; placing Stage~1 before it changes AP by a further \tMinusBzTMean{} points, within seed noise. We verified the restriction empirically as well as analytically: during Stage~2, $s^{(1)}$ on a fixed probe batch did not change at all, and no parameter outside $\mathrm{mlp}_1$ was updated, which matches Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad}.

\textbf{Division of labour between the stages.} The two ablations describe stages with different and largely disjoint effects. Stage~1 acts before the branch point and improves the classification output that UCF-Crime is headlined on, while leaving XD-Violence AP effectively unchanged (\xdStageOneMean{} points). Stage~2 acts after it and improves the semantic output that XD-Violence is headlined on, while leaving the classification output unchanged. Each dataset's gain is therefore traceable to one stage, and we do not claim synergy: the evidence supports two independent, non-interfering refinements at two positions, not a combination that is more than its parts. The practical consequence is that either stage can be adopted alone depending on which output a deployment reads.

\subsection{Efficiency}
\label{sec:robust}

\input{tables/tab_complexity}

Table~\ref{tab:complexity} reports inference cost on an \cxGpu{} per window of 256 snippets. The two stages contribute very differently. Stage~1 is the only source of overhead: the low-rank variation residual adds \pvrParams{} parameters (about 1\% of the trainable model), and the difference computation and sparse graph add \cxUcfLatRel\% latency on UCF-Crime and \cxXdLatRel\% on XD-Violence, with at most \cxUcfMemDelta{}~MB extra peak memory. Stage~2 adds nothing at inference: it changes only the values of an adapter VadCLIP already evaluates, so the inference graph, parameter count, and cost are those of the Stage~1 model. Training cost is similarly modest, since Stage~2 updates \mlpOneParams{} parameters for three epochs on a frozen network. In absolute terms both models process a 256-snippet window in under 30~ms on a laptop GPU, excluding the frozen CLIP feature extraction that VadCLIP and our method share.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results support a narrow but useful claim. Neither stage changes VadCLIP's architecture or the losses of its existing outputs; each changes a position: where temporal information enters the model, and which subgraph an additional gradient may reach. This makes the method straightforward to adopt. Stage~1 is a drop-in input refinement that starts from the original representation by construction, and Stage~2 is a short post-training step on an existing checkpoint that is guaranteed not to alter the classification scores a deployed system may already rely on, for example for alert thresholds calibrated on $s^{(1)}$. The gains are moderate in absolute terms, particularly on UCF-Crime, but they are positive on every matched seed and accompanied by controls that rule out the obvious alternative explanations. The limitations are equally clear: all results are on CLIP features and a single base architecture, and the official-script comparison covers XD-Violence only. The underlying principle, preserving information before an irreversible aggregation and confining an auxiliary objective to the parameters downstream of a branch, is not specific to VadCLIP and may apply to other detectors with fixed-length resampling or multiple outputs.
